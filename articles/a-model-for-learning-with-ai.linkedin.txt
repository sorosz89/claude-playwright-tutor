I spent a session building a small test-automation learning repo with an AI assistant. Along the way, I kept pushing the process itself: brainstorm before building, scaffold before solving, understand before implementing, drill before trusting it stuck.

When I stepped back and asked the assistant to map that process onto real research, the answer surprised me in a good way: none of it was new. Every step mapped onto learning science that's decades old. "Learning with an AI tutor" isn't a new category of thing — it's old pedagogy, with a tutor that's finally cheap enough to run for one person at a time.

Here's the model, with the research behind each stage.

WHY BOTHER WITH AN AI TUTOR AT ALL

Benjamin Bloom asked this exact question in 1984. He found that one-to-one tutoring, combined with mastery learning, moved the average student about two standard deviations above classroom instruction — good enough that the average tutored student outperformed 98% of a control class. His own conclusion: one-to-one tutoring is "too costly for most societies to bear" at scale (Bloom, 1984).

An AI tutor doesn't close that gap automatically. None of the seven stages below happen just because a model is involved — each one is a design choice. But AI does remove the cost constraint that made one-to-one tutoring unscalable in the first place.

THE MODEL — SEVEN STAGES

1. Anchor on goals before deliverables
Decide what capability you want to end up with before deciding what to build. This is backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, "Understanding by Design"): identify the desired result first, then what would count as evidence you got there, and only then the activities.

2. Scaffold from what you already half-know
New material lands best just above your current ability, anchored to context you already have. This is Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development. "Scaffolding" is the name for a tutor building on what a learner already has — Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) described six functions a good tutor performs, one of which is explicitly frustration control. That detail matters later.

3. Model, then fade
Show a complete, correct example before asking for an extension of the pattern. This is the worked-example effect (Sweller & Cooper, 1985; Sweller, 1988): for a novice, studying a full worked solution teaches more per minute than being handed an open problem, because solving from scratch burns effort on search instead of understanding. The same idea, staged more gradually, is cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989: model, coach, scaffold, fade) and, in literacy instruction, gradual release of responsibility — "I do, we do, you do" (Pearson & Gallagher, 1983).

4. Guide, don't abandon
"Figure it out yourself" is not automatically good pedagogy. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark's widely-cited 2006 paper, "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work," argues that minimally guided approaches underperform guided instruction because they ignore how working memory and expertise actually develop. The advantage of strong guidance only fades once a learner already has enough prior knowledge to self-guide — which echoes that "frustration control" function from stage 2.

Worth naming the nuance honestly: productive-failure research (Kapur) finds that genuine struggle before explicit instruction can deepen conceptual transfer. The tension isn't settled science — it's a design choice about how much struggle is useful before you reveal the answer.

5. Verify against reality, not against how confident the AI sounds
A claim that sounds well-researched is not the same as a claim that's true. In my session, a research pass returned a plausible but fictitious detail — and it only got caught by checking it against the one source that actually mattered, not by re-reading the claim more carefully. The fix wasn't "think harder about the AI's answer." It was "go verify it somewhere else."

6. Separate knowing, doing, and recalling
Reading an explanation, writing code that uses it, and recalling it later unprompted are three different cognitive activities. Conflating them hides whether any of them actually happened. Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 research on the testing effect found that actively recalling material produces better long-term retention than re-reading it — re-reading mostly builds a false sense of familiarity, not retrievable knowledge. That's the case for treating "read the explanation," "do the exercise," and "get quizzed on it later, with no notes" as three separate steps, not one.

7. Close the loop with reflection
Periodically step back from the artifact and ask what the process itself just taught you, independent of what you built. This article is that step.

APPLYING THIS TO YOUR NEXT AI-ASSISTED LEARNING PROJECT

- Write down the capability you want before the thing you'll build.
- Ground new material in something you already know — point the AI at your own prior work, not a blank prompt.
- Ask for one fully-worked example before you ask for an open exercise.
- Decide up front how much to withhold: bounded exercises over open-ended ones at first, hints before answers, and a real point where the answer gets revealed rather than withheld forever.
- Don't trust an AI's claims by how confident they sound. Check them against a real source, especially before they get baked into something you'll teach from.
- Test your recall of anything you learn separately, later, without the source material open. If you can't recall it unprompted, you don't know it yet — you recognized it.
- When the thing is done, spend five minutes on what the process itself taught you.
